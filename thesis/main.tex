\begin{abstract}
We implement and empirically evaluate the polynomial-time algorithm of
Chen, Wein, and Zhang (arXiv:2511.04345, November~2025) for the
next-to-shortest path (NSP) problem in directed graphs with strictly
positive edge weights --- the first polynomial-time algorithm for this
problem after nearly thirty years as an open question. Our C++20
implementation follows the paper's three-stage pipeline: a reduction to
an $(s,t)$-straight digraph by iterative folding of off-shortest-path
vertices, a further reduction to a strictly $(s,t)$-layered digraph
(subdividing layer-skipping forward edges and removing forward-going
back-edges while recording their candidate paths), and finally a
$6$-tuple enumeration on the layered graph that invokes a
$2$-vertex-disjoint paths subroutine in directed acyclic graphs.

Correctness is validated by two independent oracles --- a brute-force
simple-path enumerator (capped at $|V|\le 13$) and Yen's $k$-shortest
simple-paths algorithm used as an NSP solver --- and by a
differential-fuzzing harness that records \emph{zero} NSP-cost
mismatches across more than $35{,}000$ random instances ($|V|\le 14$) and
$100\%$ agreement with Yen on every benign benchmark instance up to
$|V|=50$. Empirically the CWZ algorithm is several orders of magnitude
slower than Yen on the four benign random families we sweep (where
shortest paths are typically unique), but on a deliberately-constructed
adversarial diamond-chain family with $2^{k}$ distinct shortest paths
the picture reverses: at $k=15$ ($|V|=46$) Yen takes $59$~seconds while
CWZ completes in $1.4$~milliseconds, a ${\sim}41{,}000\times$ ratio that
grows exponentially with $k$; and with a practical enumeration cap Yen
returns the wrong answer entirely on the largest instances while CWZ
stays correct. This is, to our knowledge, the first empirical study of
the CWZ algorithm.
\end{abstract}
